About

A practice built around the moments that test us most

For over two decades, I have worked with individuals and couples navigating the experiences that most profoundly test us — relationship rupture, navigating early adulthood, grief, loss, midlife transition, and the complex emotions that so often accompany major life change. My practice is built on the belief that these moments, as painful as they are, also carry the possibility of deep transformation.

My approach is rooted in a fundamental truth about human psychology: all emotional suffering, and all healing, happens in relationship — with ourselves, with our past, and with the people we love. This conviction shapes everything about how I work.

Rather than applying a single methodology, I draw from a range of evidence-based approaches, always in service of what each person actually needs. The goal is not simply symptom relief, but lasting change — a genuinely different relationship with yourself and those in your life.

Clients consistently describe therapy with me as feeling both safe enough to explore difficult places, and rigorous enough to actually get somewhere.

Couples Therapy

Our closest relationships hold a mirror to everything unresolved within us

Patterns from our families of origin, unspoken needs, early experiences of rupture or abandonment — these don't disappear when we enter a relationship. They show up in it, often in ways that feel confusing or disproportionate in the moment. What looks like a conflict about communication is frequently something older and deeper being played out between two people who genuinely care about each other.

My work with couples focuses on two things: helping you recognize the patterns that are keeping you stuck, and building the communication tools and emotional fluency to replace them. This is not about assigning blame. It is about creating enough safety and clarity that both people can be honest — and heard.

Couples therapy may be a good fit if you are navigating:

  • Communication patterns that keep cycling without resolution
  • Emotional distance or disconnection
  • Conflict that feels disproportionate or repetitive
  • Life transitions that have strained the relationship
  • A desire to deepen intimacy before problems take root

The Measure of a Man

What does it mean to be a man today? The traditional measures of success — achievement, financial power, stoicism, self-sufficiency — no longer tell the whole story. And yet men are now expected to be financially formidable, physically fit, and emotionally evolved — a tension that is rarely acknowledged and almost never examined.

This group will explore where these ideas of identity came from and whether they still serve us — looking honestly at achievement, societal expectations, the loss of male friendships, and expanding emotional vocabulary so that we can communicate more honestly and connect more deeply.
